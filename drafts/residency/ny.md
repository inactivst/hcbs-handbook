# New York — residency agreement and involuntary discharge

State: New York (NY)
Retrieved / written: 2026-07-30
Researcher: agent (research pass per `../residency-research-scope.md`)

**Agency:** New York State Office for People With Developmental Disabilities (OPWDD).
**Regulations:** Title 14 NYCRR, Chapter XIV (OPWDD). Statutory base: NYS Mental Hygiene
Law Article 16.

**Facility categories covered by the answers below.** OPWDD-certified residential
settings: Individualized Residential Alternatives (IRAs), certified Community Residences
(CRs), and Family Care Homes (FCHs). 14 NYCRR Part 633 (which contains the objection
process) applies to **all** facilities and services operated or certified by OPWDD, which
also reaches ICFs/IID and day programs. The **occupancy agreement** requirement is
narrower: it applies to HCBS-funded provider-owned or controlled residential settings, and
14 NYCRR 636-2.4(d)(3) expressly excludes ICFs/IID from being HCBS settings — so an
ICF/IID resident gets the 633.12 objection process but is **not** covered by the occupancy
agreement rule. Do not carry the occupancy-agreement answer into an ICF/IID question.

**Headline.** New York is an *administrative* state, and on the stay question it is
stronger than Minnesota. But it does **not** set any minimum number of days of advance
notice before an involuntary discharge — the protection is procedural, not a clock.

---

## 1. Is a written residency/admission agreement required? Which regulation?

**Yes.** 14 NYCRR § 636-2.4(b)(1) requires a legally enforceable agreement in
OPWDD-operated or provider owned or controlled residential settings. OPWDD calls this an
**"occupancy agreement"** and implements it through **23-ADM-05, Occupancy Agreement
Guidelines** (issued 7/17/2023), which names IRAs, Family Care Homes and certified
Community Residences as the settings subject to the requirement.

OPWDD does not mandate a particular form. 23-ADM-05 Section II lists what the agreement
must document: rent obligations; the individual's rights (with references to at minimum
42 CFR § 441.301(c)(4) and 14 NYCRR §§ 633.12 and 636-2.4); provider responsibilities;
**"Ending the Occupancy Agreement"**; term; and signatures. Section III lists prohibited
terms (rights waivers, visitor restrictions, eviction for increased acuity where supports
were available, requirements to work or to attend the provider's programs).

Two carve-outs from the paperwork, not from the protection: providers with an existing HUD
lease, or with a lease under OPWDD's Housing Subsidy program (22-ADM-03) / FOFILLS, do not
need a separate occupancy agreement — but per 23-ADM-05 they "are still required to
provide the same protections and due process to residents under the 14 NYCRR § 633.12
regulations."

A person cannot be punished for not signing: 23-ADM-05 Section IV — providers "may not
treat a failure or inability to sign an occupancy agreement as a basis to terminate an
individual's services, initiate or propose a discharge or relocation, or to ask an
individual to move out of a residence."

## 2. Does landlord/tenant law apply, or is there a carve-out?

**There is an explicit carve-out, and OPWDD states it in writing.** New York took the
second branch of 42 CFR 441.301(c)(4)(vi)(A): rather than treating certified settings as
tenancies, it substitutes the 633.12 objection process as the "comparable" eviction
protection.

From 23-ADM-05, Discussion Section I:

> OPWDD does not consider certified settings to be subject to landlord-tenant laws as the
> individual is protected by processes in 14 NYCRR § 633.12, but providers should consult
> with their own legal counsel on whether these laws may apply to a particular residential
> setting and create additional rights for individuals or obligations for the provider.

And in Section III (Prohibited Terms):

> OPWDD considers certified settings to be outside the scope of state and local landlord
> tenant laws and, therefore, are not subject to those laws. Regardless, the procedures in
> 14 NYCRR § 633.12 provide the necessary protections from eviction required by the
> federal rule. However, the occupancy agreement should not contain any statement that an
> individual waives the ability to raise the potential applicability of state or local
> landlord tenant laws in any court or administrative proceeding.

That last sentence is worth keeping: OPWDD asserts the carve-out but forbids providers
from making the resident waive the argument. Non-certified, non-provider-controlled rentals
are outside the ADM and, in OPWDD's words, "are likely subject to landlord-tenant laws."

Caution: this is OPWDD's administrative position (tier 2), not a judicial holding. No New
York court decision resolving whether an IRA resident is a tenant under RPAPL was located
in this pass; that question was not run to ground and should not be represented as settled.

## 3. Notice period(s) before an involuntary discharge; short-notice exception and who approves it

**Could not confirm any minimum advance-notice period.** This is a genuine absence, and it
is the weak spot in New York's scheme.

- 14 NYCRR § 633.12 requires notice **of the objection mechanism** and (for waiver service
  changes) notice of the proposed change, but sets **no number of days** of advance notice
  before a proposed discharge from a certified residence.
- 14 NYCRR § 636-2.4 sets no notice period.
- 14 NYCRR Part 686 (community residences / IRA certification) has **no** admission,
  discharge, transfer or notice section at all — its sections run 686.1–686.17 plus a
  glossary, and none of them address discharge.
- 23-ADM-05 requires the occupancy agreement to *describe the process*, and to give
  examples of situations that may lead to termination, but does not impose a day count.

What New York does specify are the **clocks inside the objection process**, once an
objection is filed (14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(8)):

| Step | Timeframe | Track |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing scheduled after formal written objection | within 5 days | discharge/placement (i) |
| Notice to parties of the hearing | no less than 10 days | discharge/placement (i) |
| Hearing officer's written decision | within 14 days of hearing | discharge/placement (i) |
| Appeal to the Commissioner | within 10 days | discharge/placement (i) |
| Commissioner's final decision | within 14 days of receipt of appeal | discharge/placement (i) |
| Window to file the objection after notice of failed informal resolution | 14 days, and the service may not be reduced/suspended/discontinued during it | HCBS waiver service (ii) |
| Expedited hearing scheduled (immediate health/safety risk) | within 7 days of the Commissioner's determination | HCBS waiver service (ii) |
| Notice of an expedited hearing | at least 3 days, absent good cause | HCBS waiver service (ii) |

**Short-notice / emergency exception, and who approves it.** For discharge or relocation,
14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(8)(i)(e)(3) permits a health-and-safety move, but while an objection
is pending, relocation or discharge "shall only take place with the commissioner's
approval" — i.e. the **OPWDD Commissioner**, not the provider and not the regional office.
For the expedited *service* track, the request goes to the Commissioner, who decides
whether an expedited hearing is warranted. OPWDD's model occupancy agreement also treats
short temporary moves for health/welfare/safety as "emergency moves," with the objection
right attaching if the person stays in the new residence more than 60 days.

## 4. Is there an appeal, and is it ADMINISTRATIVE or JUDICIAL?

**ADMINISTRATIVE**, with judicial review afterward.

The forum is OPWDD's own objection-to-services process under 14 NYCRR § 633.12: informal
resolution at the agency, then a **formal written objection to the DDSO/regional office
director**, then a hearing before a **hearing officer appointed by that director**, then an
appeal to the **OPWDD Commissioner**. Both the discharge/placement track and the HCBS
waiver service-reduction track end at the Commissioner.

Judicial review exists but only as a second stage: 14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(11) — "The
commissioner's decision is the final administrative remedy available and may be appealed in
accordance with the provisions of article 78 of the Civil Practice Law and Rules."

Naming it precisely, because the scope doc asks: this is **not** a court defense to an
eviction (California's model), and it is **not** merely an internal provider grievance. The
informal step *is* internal to the provider, but the objecting party can escalate past it to
a state-appointed hearing officer and then to the Commissioner, with an Article 78
proceeding in Supreme Court available at the end. Anyone may be represented, including by
counsel (633.12(a)(7)), and Mental Hygiene Legal Service may itself initiate an objection
(633.12(a)(4)).

Separate and not to be confused: OPWDD care management (CCO/Health Home) determinations run
through the Medicaid **Fair Hearing** system before OTDA under 18 NYCRR Part 358, with its
own aid-continuing rules. That is a different forum from 633.12 and governs enrollment /
care-management decisions, not a provider's discharge from a certified residence. Do not
merge the two.

## 5. Must the NOTICE ITSELF state the appeal right?

**Partly yes — and yes in OPWDD's own written instruction to providers, though the
regulation's text is weaker than Minnesota's on the discharge track specifically.**

- **HCBS waiver service reduction/suspension/discontinuance — yes, in the regulation.**
  14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(5): when an agency proposes to reduce, suspend, or discontinue a
  waiver service, it "shall, in a form and format approved by the commissioner, advise the
  person, and his or her advocate and service coordinator ... of the proposed changes and
  of the mechanism for resolving an objection to the proposed changes." So the notice of
  the action must carry the objection mechanism.
- **Discharge from a certified residence — the regulation ties the duty to admission and
  to changes**, not explicitly to the discharge notice: the same paragraph requires that
  the person and representatives "shall be advised of the mechanism to resolve an
  objection: upon admission to a facility or enrollment in HCBS waiver services, as changes
  occur, and upon any substantive amendment to this section."
- **OPWDD's own directive closes the gap at tier 2.** The September 9, 2020 Deputy
  Commissioner memo *Due Process and Inappropriate Discharge from Residential Programs and
  Services* states that required actions include "providing notice of the provider's
  intended action and notifying the individual of their right to object and to have a
  hearing."
- 23-ADM-05 also requires the occupancy agreement itself to carry a statement that 633.12
  applies and that the person may formally object, plus contact details for Mental Hygiene
  Legal Service and Disability Rights New York.

Honest framing for advocacy: New York requires the *agreement* to state the objection
right, requires the *waiver-service* notice to state it, and OPWDD tells providers in
writing that a discharge notice must state it — but the regulation does not spell out, as
Minn. Stat. § 245D.10 subd. 3a(d) does, that the discharge notice must contain the appeal
right and the stay right. The ask "put it in the notice" is still live in New York; the
ask is narrower than in a state with nothing at all.

## 6. Is there a stay / "services continue pending appeal"?

**Yes, on both tracks, and on the service track it is automatic and absolute.** This is
New York's strongest feature.

- **Discharge / placement track**, 14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(8)(i)(e)(3): a health-and-safety
  change is not precluded, "However, while an objection to placement or discharge is
  undergoing administrative review, relocation or discharge shall only take place with the
  commissioner's approval." Not a stay the person must request — a bar on the provider,
  liftable only by the Commissioner. Paragraph (e)(2) adds that "every effort feasible
  shall be made to maintain the person in at least his or her current level of
  programming."
- **HCBS waiver service track**, 14 NYCRR § 633.12(a)(8)(ii)(e): "During the period that an
  objection is undergoing administrative review (including an expedited review), the agency
  shall not reduce, suspend or discontinue the HCBS waiver service(s) at issue, unless
  otherwise agreed to by both parties." Clause (ii)(b) also freezes the service during the
  14-day window in which the objection may be filed.

Compare to Minnesota, where the person must *seek* a temporary order staying termination.
In New York the freeze is the default and the burden runs the other way.

## Also worth knowing: what counts as a discharge

New York has closed a common evasion. Refusing to take someone back after a hospital stay
or a home visit is a discharge, and triggers 633.12. From the September 9, 2020 OPWDD memo:

> "Regardless of the purported reason, a provider's refusal to allow an individual to
> return to his or her residence or other service program is considered a discharge."

The same memo states that a bad discharge is an enforcement matter: "A provider's attempt
to inappropriately discharge an individual is a violation of 14 NYCRR 633.12, as well as
Article 16 of the NYS Mental Hygiene Law. In accordance with the OPWDD Accountability
Initiative, OPWDD will impose appropriate fines for any such violations and may take
additional adverse certification actions as needed."
